Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19)

COVID-19 is a disease caused by a virus named SARS-CoV-2. It can be very contagious and spreads quickly.

This page summarizes public guidance from the CDC COVID-19 page (opens in a new tab). It is general information, not a diagnosis or a treatment plan. For Orange County programs, see the county COVID-19 page (opens in a new tab).

About COVID-19

COVID-19 most often causes respiratory symptoms that can feel much like a cold, the flu, or pneumonia. It can also affect parts of the body beyond the lungs. Most people have mild symptoms, but some people become severely ill.

How it spreads

COVID-19 spreads when an infected person breathes out droplets and very small particles that contain the virus. Other people can breathe those in, or the droplets can land on their eyes, nose, or mouth. In some cases, droplets can contaminate surfaces.

Anyone who is infected can spread it, even without symptoms. In some situations, people can also spread it to animals.

Variants

The virus changes over time. Those changes can produce variants that spread differently. Slowing spread—by protecting yourself and others—can slow the development of new variants.

Source: CDC, About COVID-19 (opens in a new tab) (reviewed June 13, 2024).

Symptoms

Symptoms range from mild to severe illness. They may appear 2–14 days after exposure. They can start mild, and some people go on to have more severe symptoms. The list below does not include every possible symptom. Symptoms can change with new variants and can differ by vaccination status.

  • Fever or chills
  • Cough
  • Shortness of breath or difficulty breathing
  • Sore throat
  • Congestion or runny nose
  • New loss of taste or smell
  • Fatigue
  • Muscle or body aches
  • Headache
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Diarrhea

You cannot tell flu and COVID-19 apart by symptoms alone. Some lab tests can check for both at the same time.

If you have respiratory symptoms that are not better explained by another cause, stay home and away from others, including people you live with who are not sick. If you have risk factors for severe illness, seek health care promptly for testing and treatment.

When to seek emergency help

Look for these emergency warning signs. This is not a complete list. Call a medical provider about any symptom that is severe or worrying.

  • Trouble breathing
  • Persistent pain or pressure in the chest
  • New confusion
  • Inability to wake or stay awake
  • Pale, gray, or blue lips, nail beds, or skin, depending on skin tone

If someone has any of these signs, call 911 or call ahead to your local emergency facility. Tell the operator you are seeking care for someone who has or may have COVID-19.

Source: CDC, Symptoms of COVID-19 (opens in a new tab) (March 10, 2025).

People at higher risk

Some people are more likely to get very sick from COVID-19, including people who are older, immunocompromised, have certain disabilities, or have underlying health conditions. Older adults are at the highest risk. CDC reports that more than 81% of COVID-19 deaths occur in people over age 65, and that deaths among people over 65 are 97 times higher than among people ages 18–29. Risk also rises as the number of underlying conditions increases.

Some people are at increased risk because of where they live or work, or because they cannot get health care. This includes many people from racial and ethnic minority groups and people with disabilities.

If you have symptoms consistent with COVID-19 and you are 50 or older, or you have a condition that raises your risk, you may be eligible for treatment. Treatment works best when it starts within 5–7 days of the first symptoms.

Conditions that can increase risk

The conditions below are in alphabetical order, not in order of risk. This is not a complete list. A condition that is not listed can still raise risk. Talk with a healthcare provider about your own risk.

  • Cancer
  • Cerebrovascular disease, including stroke
  • Chronic kidney disease, at any stage
  • Chronic liver disease
  • Chronic lung disease, including moderate to severe asthma and COPD
  • Cystic fibrosis
  • Dementia or other neurological conditions
  • Diabetes (type 1 or type 2)
  • Certain disabilities
  • Heart conditions, and possibly high blood pressure
  • Hemoglobin blood disorders, including sickle cell disease
  • HIV
  • A weakened immune system
  • Some mental health conditions, including depression
  • Overweight and obesity
  • Physical inactivity
  • Pregnancy
  • Smoking, current or former
  • Solid organ or blood stem cell transplant
  • Substance use disorders
  • Tuberculosis

Children and teens can also get very sick, especially with medical complexity, genetic, neurologic, or metabolic conditions, congenital heart disease, obesity, diabetes, asthma or chronic lung disease, sickle cell disease, or a weakened immune system. Parents of children ages 6 months to 17 years should discuss vaccination with a healthcare provider.

Source: CDC, People with Certain Medical Conditions and COVID-19 (opens in a new tab) (updated October 8, 2026).

How to protect yourself and others

CDC recommends these core steps for everyone:

  • Stay up to date with COVID-19 vaccines. Vaccinated people can still get infected. Staying up to date lowers the risk of severe illness, hospitalization, and death.
  • Practice good hygiene.
  • Take steps for cleaner air.

When you are sick

  • Stay home and away from others, including people you live with who are not sick, if you have respiratory symptoms.
  • CDC’s respiratory virus guidance explains when you can go back to your normal activities.
  • If you have risk factors for severe illness, seek testing and treatment promptly. Treatment needs to start within a few days of when symptoms begin.

Additional steps you can choose

  • Wearing a mask and keeping distance from others can lower the chance of spreading or catching COVID-19.
  • Testing can help you decide what to do next, including treatment and steps to avoid spreading the virus.

These steps matter most when respiratory viruses are causing a lot of illness in the community, when you or people around you are at higher risk, or when someone was recently exposed, is sick, or is recovering.

Source: CDC, How to Protect Yourself and Others (opens in a new tab) (March 10, 2025).

Testing

A viral test looks for a current infection with SARS-CoV-2, using a specimen from the nose or mouth. Testing can help you decide on treatment and on steps to avoid spreading the virus.

Two main types of viral tests

  • Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), including PCR, are more likely to detect the virus than antigen tests. A healthcare provider usually collects the sample and sends it to a lab. Results can take up to 3 days. Some NAATs are done on site and return faster.
  • Antigen tests are rapid tests, often with results in 15–30 minutes. A positive result is reliable. Antigen tests are less likely to detect the virus than NAATs, especially when you have no symptoms, so one negative antigen test cannot rule out infection. At-home self-tests are antigen tests. Follow the package instructions.

FDA recommends 2 negative antigen tests for people with symptoms, or 3 for people without symptoms, done 48 hours apart, before you can be confident you do not have COVID-19. A single NAAT can confirm an antigen result.

Choosing a test after a recent infection

  • No COVID-19, or no positive test in the past 90 days: you may use a NAAT or an antigen test. If an antigen test is negative, repeat it using FDA recommendations.
  • First positive test was 30 days ago or less, and you have symptoms: use an antigen test, and repeat a negative result.
  • First positive test was 30 days ago or less, and you do not have symptoms: testing is not recommended to look for a new infection.
  • First positive test was 31–90 days ago: use an antigen test whether or not you have symptoms, and repeat a negative result.

After a positive test, some tests—especially NAATs—can stay positive for up to 90 days. Reinfection can also happen in that window, so a new positive result can be hard to interpret. Ask a healthcare provider if you are unsure.

Where to get a test

  • Buy self-tests online or at pharmacies and retail stores. Health insurance may reimburse the cost. FDA lists authorized tests.
  • Visit a pharmacy, health center, or other community testing site. Some offer low- or no-cost testing. Your local health department may also have free tests.
  • Ask a doctor or healthcare provider about other options.
  • If you have a disability, the Disability Information and Access Line can help you get a test or find a location. CDC’s testing page has that contact information.

What the result means

Positive: the virus was detected. Take steps to avoid spreading it, watch your symptoms, and get emergency care for warning signs. If you are at higher risk, seek treatment right away.

Negative: the test did not detect the virus. That does not always rule out infection. If you used an antigen test, follow FDA guidance on repeat testing. You may have tested too early, or you may have another illness. Keep using steps that lower the risk from respiratory viruses, and contact a healthcare provider if you have questions.

Source: CDC, Testing for COVID-19 (opens in a new tab) (March 10, 2025).

Treatment

If you have COVID-19 and are more likely to get very sick, medications can lower the risk of hospitalization and death. Do not delay: treatments must start within 5–7 days after symptoms begin. You do not need a positive test to start treatment. If you are at high risk, talk with a healthcare provider as soon as you get sick, even if symptoms are mild.

People more likely to get very sick include:

  • Older adults, especially ages 75 and older, with risk rising as age increases
  • People who are unvaccinated or not up to date on COVID-19 vaccination
  • People with certain medical conditions, such as chronic lung disease, heart disease, or a weakened immune system

FDA has authorized or approved antiviral medications for mild to moderate COVID-19 in people who are more likely to get very sick. Antivirals target the virus so it stops multiplying. They are not a substitute for vaccination. Some treatments have side effects or interact with other medicines. Paxlovid is more likely to interact with other medicines than other COVID-19 antivirals, and it can cause a metallic taste that goes away when you stop taking it. Most people can still take it. Check interactions with a healthcare provider or pharmacist.

If you are hospitalized, providers may use other medicines depending on how sick you are. People with a weakened immune system who have had antiviral treatment and still have symptoms may be offered additional treatment, including convalescent plasma.

To find testing and treatment, or a pharmacy that can fill a prescription, use the CDC treatment page (opens in a new tab) or call 1-800-232-0233 (TTY 1-888-720-7489). Paxlovid and Lagevrio patient assistance programs can lower cost for some people who are uninsured, underinsured, on Medicaid, or on Medicare. Call your provider and insurer to confirm coverage.

People have been seriously harmed, and some have died, after taking products that are not approved to treat or prevent COVID-19, even when those products were approved for other uses. Talk with a healthcare provider before taking medication for COVID-19.

Preventive antibody for some immunocompromised people

FDA has authorized pemivibart (Pemgarda) for people who are moderately or severely immunocompromised, ages 12 and older, and who weigh at least 88 pounds. It is given as a single intravenous infusion and may be repeated every 3 months if protection is still needed. It can be given at least 2 weeks after a COVID-19 vaccine. It is not a treatment for COVID-19. Ask a healthcare provider whether it is appropriate.

Mild illness and rebound

Most people have mild illness and can recover at home. Over-the-counter medicines such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen can help with symptoms.

COVID-19 rebound is a return of symptoms, or a new positive test, 3–7 days after you recover or after you test negative. Rebound can happen whether or not you took an antiviral. Symptoms are usually mild and resolve in a few days. For people at high risk, the benefits of treatment outweigh the risk of rebound. Contact a healthcare provider, community health center, or health department if you think you have rebound.

Source: CDC, Types of COVID-19 Treatment (opens in a new tab) (updated February 5, 2026).

COVID-19 vaccines

People who are up to date with their COVID-19 vaccine have a lower risk of severe illness. Vaccines available in the United States protect against serious illness, hospitalization, and death. Protection is best when you stay up to date.

Staying up to date is especially important if you are older or have a health condition that raises your risk. People with a weakened immune system may not be fully protected even when they are up to date, and some may benefit from additional doses of the 2026–2027 COVID-19 vaccine.

Free COVID-19 vaccines may be available through private health insurance, Medicare, Medicaid, public health safety-net programs, and CDC’s Vaccines for Children program. To find a location, go to vaccinefinder.org (opens in a new tab).

Recommendations can change by age, pregnancy, and immune status. CDC has posted interim clinical considerations for the 2026–2027 COVID-19 vaccines. Check CDC before you schedule a dose, and talk with a healthcare provider if you are pregnant, want to become pregnant, are breastfeeding, or are immunocompromised.

Sources: CDC, COVID-19 Vaccines (opens in a new tab) (July 30, 2025) and People with Certain Medical Conditions (opens in a new tab) (updated October 8, 2026).

Long COVID

Some people develop post-COVID conditions, also called Long COVID, including people who had mild symptoms or none. CDC describes Long COVID as an infection-associated chronic condition that is present for at least 3 months after SARS-CoV-2 infection. It can be continuous, come and go, or worsen over time, and it can affect one or more organ systems.

Long COVID can happen to anyone who gets infected. CDC reports higher risk among women, people with underlying health conditions, people who had more severe COVID-19, people who did not get a COVID-19 vaccine, and people of Hispanic ethnicity. Commonly reported symptoms include fatigue, trouble concentrating, and exhaustion after activity. More than 200 symptoms have been described. That list is not a way to diagnose yourself.

The most direct way to lower the chance of Long COVID is to lower the chance of infection: stay up to date on vaccination and use the core prevention steps above. Talk with a healthcare provider about symptoms that continue or return after the acute illness.

Sources: CDC, About COVID-19 (opens in a new tab) and CDC Science and the Public Health Approach to Long COVID (opens in a new tab).

Reinfection

Reinfection means you were infected, recovered, and then got infected again. You can be reinfected more than once. Reinfections are most often mild, but severe illness can still happen, and you can spread the virus to others.

After COVID-19, the immune response can protect against another infection for several months, then that protection fades. People with weakened immune systems may have a limited response, or none. Protection against severe illness generally lasts longer than protection against infection. New variants that can evade existing immunity raise the chance of reinfection. Reinfection within several weeks is possible but rare.

Core prevention steps still apply after you have had COVID-19. Staying up to date on vaccines and starting treatment within a few days of symptoms lower the risk of severe illness. A positive test in the first 90 days after an infection can be hard to interpret. Use the testing guidance in the testing section above.

Source: CDC, About Reinfection (opens in a new tab) (June 14, 2024).
